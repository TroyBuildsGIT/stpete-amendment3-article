St. Pete's Property Tax Shakeup: What Amendment 3 Means for Buyers, Sellers & Airbnb Investors

By Troy Nowak, Broker Associate · Mangrove Bay Realty · mangrovebayrealty.com

Florida voters decide on November 3 whether to rewrite the property tax rules that fund St. Petersburg's police, fire, parks, and libraries. The measure is called Amendment 3, and if it passes, it lands on St. Pete harder than almost anywhere in Pinellas County. Here's the straight talk for anyone buying, selling, or running a short-term rental here.

What the amendment actually does

It is not a full elimination of property taxes, and it is not Governor DeSantis's original plan. The legislature passed HJR 1-F in a special session on June 2, and it is now on the November 3 ballot as Amendment 3. It needs 60 percent approval to pass.

  • The homestead exemption on non-school taxes jumps from about $51,000 to $150,000 in 2027, then $250,000 in 2028, indexed to inflation after that.
  • School taxes stay untouched, with the existing $25,000 exemption.
  • New Florida residents who move in after 2026 get a reduced $50,000 exemption for their first five years.
  • The annual assessment-growth cap on non-homestead property — rentals, second homes, commercial — drops from 10 percent to 5 percent.

There is no state fund to backfill the lost local revenue. That hole is the whole story for St. Pete.

The St. Pete math

City officials are bracing for a serious hit. Councilman Copley Gerdes told WFLA the city expects to collect about $248 million in ad valorem taxes in 2027, and the phased exemption could cost St. Petersburg $50 to $70 million of that — money that, as he put it, "barely covers police and fire."

The key fact: St. Petersburg's fiscal policy sends property tax revenue to public safety first. In FY2026, roughly $174 million went to police and $29 million to fire rescue. Everything else — parks, libraries, recreation, planning, code enforcement — is paid from other revenue. Cut the property tax base and those services are the first to shrink.

Administrators are already preparing a fire assessment study, one of the few legal ways to replace lost revenue. The city projects a $33 million General Fund gap in fiscal 2028 and another $21 million the year after. Across Pinellas, the Pinellas Suncoast Transit Authority could lose $15 million in 2028, rising to $22 million, which threatens the Jolly Trolley and other routes.

Who wins and who pays

Longtime St. Pete homeowners are the clear winners. The Pinellas County Property Appraiser estimates a qualifying homeowner could save about $1,200 in 2027 and $2,400 in 2028 using the countywide average non-school rate. On a typical St. Pete bill, non-school levies are roughly 60 percent of the total, so the savings are real.

But the relief is a transfer, not a discount. The burden shifts onto renters, commercial property, and second homes — and St. Pete has a lot of all three. The 5 percent cap on non-homestead assessment growth is the sleeper provision for investors: in a market climbing 8 percent a year, a rental's taxable value gets held down, which sounds friendly until you realize the city will raise millage rates or fees to cover the gap. Either way, the cost lands on the property.

What this means for Airbnb and short-term rental investors

This is where I live, so I'll be blunt. St. Petersburg already restricts short-term rentals hard: in most residential zoning you can rent under 30 days no more than three times per rolling year unless you hold a rare Resort Facilities Overlay. Guests pay a stacked 13 percent tax — 6 percent state sales, 1 percent Pinellas surtax, 6 percent tourist development. Flood and wind insurance on coastal AE and VE zones can run $8,000 to $15,000 a year and erase the spread on a marginal deal.

My read as an owner and operator: Amendment 3 does not kill the STR thesis, but it raises the bar. The 5 percent non-homestead cap slows assessment creep, which helps cash flow on paper, yet the city's likely response — higher fees, a fire assessment, service cuts that make neighborhoods less attractive — works against occupancy. The winners will be well-located, legally compliant properties with strong HOAs and low insurance friction. The losers will be marginal deals underwritten on optimistic tax assumptions.

If you are buying an investment property in St. Pete right now, underwrite the tax bill two ways: with the amendment passing and with it failing. Run the numbers on the actual parcel through the Pinellas County Property Appraiser's estimator at pcpao.gov, not on a listing's advertised figure.

The honest verdict

Good for existing primary-residence homeowners, especially long-timers. Bad for the local tax base that funds the quality of life people move here for. It is a cost shift dressed up as affordability, and St. Pete — with its high municipal millage and heavy reliance on ad valorem for police and fire — feels it more than most.

If you are thinking about buying or selling in St. Petersburg before or after the November 3 vote, the smart move is to model both scenarios and talk to someone who owns rentals here, not just sells houses. That is what I do.
